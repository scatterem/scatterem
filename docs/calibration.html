Calibration

Most reconstruction problems are calibration problems. The fusion weights are functions of spatial frequency, so if the mapping from detector pixels to reciprocal space is wrong, the weights are wrong, and the result is a plausible image that is quietly incorrect. This page is the short list of things to get right.

The reciprocal pixel size, dk

dk is measured, not assumed. The bright-field disk has a known angular radius — the convergence semi-angle you supplied — so fitting its radius in pixels gives the conversion directly:

\[ dk = \frac{\alpha}{r_{\mathrm{BF}}\,\lambda} \]

with \(r_\mathrm{BF}\) the fitted radius in pixels and \(\lambda\) the relativistic electron wavelength. Dataset4dstem.from_array runs this fit during construction, and the named datasets arrive already calibrated.

The fit sweeps a set of intensity thresholds and takes the median radius, which is what makes it insensitive to where exactly you consider the disk edge to be. It refuses rather than guesses when the pattern has no compact bright region — a fit whose radius exceeds 40% of the detector usually means the input was not an averaged diffraction pattern at all.

dk = dataset.calibrate_reciprocal_from_bright_field()
print(dataset.sampling)   # (dy, dx, dky, dkx)
Counting detectors: pass a hot-pixel threshold

On an event-counting detector a single stuck pixel can dominate the maximum the threshold sweep is relative to, and the fit then locks onto that one pixel. If your detector counts, pass hot_pixel_threshold and check the fitted radius is physically sensible before trusting it.

The scan rotation

The angle between the scan axes and the detector axes has to be right, and nothing downstream can infer it: the autofocus holds rotation fixed while it fits aberrations. A wrong rotation shows up as a reconstruction that is sharp along one direction and smeared along the other, or as a fused image with directional artefacts that no amount of aberration fitting removes.

If you know it from the microscope, set it:

dataset.meta.rotation = 84.0   # degrees

If you do not, the centre-of-mass field of the bright-field disk determines it: the correct rotation is the one that makes that field a gradient — curl-free — because it is the gradient of the projected potential.

Detector orientation

Worth checking early, because it is easy to miss and it produces a specific, recognisable failure. If the detector’s axes are flipped relative to the scan, the parallax shifts come out with the wrong handedness, defocus fits toward zero, and the aberration fit reports a suspiciously small C10 regardless of the real defocus. A defocus that will not move off zero is worth suspecting a flip rather than a fitting problem.

The aberration starting guess

The autofocus is a local optimisation. It needs a starting defocus, and it raises if meta.aberrations is all zero rather than descending from nothing:

dataset.meta.aberrations.array[0] = -50.0   # C10, Angstrom; defocus == -C10
dataset.determine_aberrations_(correct_order=2)

The sign matters more than the magnitude — an estimate within a factor of two is plenty, but the wrong sign can settle into a mirror-image solution with inverted contrast. Through-focus contrast inversion in direct ptychography is physical, not a bug, so a reconstruction that looks inverted may simply be on the other side of focus.

Scan step and upsampling

scan_step is the pitch of the scan, in Ångström. The reconstruction is upsampled relative to it, by default to the factor that brings the output pitch to what the data can support (upsample="nyquist").

That upsampling is why a scale bar needs the reconstruction’s sampling, not the dataset’s:

from scatterem.vis.reconstruction_sampling import reconstruction_sampling

sampling = reconstruction_sampling(dataset, fused)

Getting this wrong overstates the bar by the upsample factor — four-fold on the paper’s Fig. 4, which is the difference between a correct figure and a wrong one.

Sanity checks worth running

  • dk is not the placeholder. If dataset.sampling[2] is exactly 1.0, the bright-field fit fell back and everything downstream is in detector pixels, not inverse Ångström.
  • The reconstruction is finite. A NaN anywhere usually means a division by a zero SSNR, which in turn means an empty channel.
  • The bright-field radius is plausible. Print it. A radius near the detector half-width, or a couple of pixels, both mean the fit found the wrong thing.
  • Both channels look sensible on their own before you read the fused result. Fusion cannot rescue a channel that failed; it will just weight it down and leave a band with nothing in it.
