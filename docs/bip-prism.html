BiP-PRISM#

What makes core-loss EELS expensive#

A core-loss STEM-EELS simulation is a double-channelling problem. The probe has to be propagated dynamically to an atom that ionizes, the ionization event has to be applied there, and the inelastically scattered wave has to be propagated dynamically from that atom to the detector. Both legs are multislice.

Written out, the conventional transition-potential multislice does this for every combination of scan position, ionized atom and transition channel. With \(P\) scan positions, \(N_a\) atoms of the ionized element and \(N_t\) channels, that is \(P N_a N_t\) exit-wave propagations. The scan is the painful factor: it is the one that grows when you ask for the atomic-resolution map you actually wanted.

The scattering matrix removes the scan#

PRISM’s idea is that the entrance leg does not depend on where the probe is. A converged probe is a sum of plane waves over the aperture, and multislice is linear, so propagating each of the \(B\) aperture beams once gives a basis \(\mathcal{S}_1\) from which the probe at any scan position is recovered as a linear combination — the coefficients are the aperture’s phase ramps. One propagation of \(B\) columns replaces \(P\) propagations of one probe.

The exit leg yields to the same argument, and this is the step that makes detector-integrated maps cheap. Everything after the transition potential is linear too, so

\[ \psi_{\text{exit}}(\boldsymbol{\rho}) \;=\; \mathcal{S}_2\Big[\,H_{n0}\sum_b c_b(\boldsymbol{\rho})\,\mathcal{S}_{1,b}\Big] \;=\; \sum_b c_b(\boldsymbol{\rho})\;\mathcal{S}_2\big[\,H_{n0}\,\mathcal{S}_{1,b}\big] . \]

The expensive operator acts on the \(B\) columns, not on the \(P\) probes, and the scan is synthesised afterwards by the same coefficients that formed it. For a detector-integrated map \(\mathcal{S}_2\) can be built once per slice as a transpose multislice of the detector-accepted output beams — by reciprocity, running the detector backwards is the same calculation as running the source forwards — and then reused across every atom in that slice. The per-scan exit propagation disappears entirely. This is the dual-matrix algorithm of Brown, Ciston & Ophus.

What is left is memory#

Having traded time for structure, the cost reappears as storage. \(\mathcal{S}_1\) is \(B\) complex arrays on the full \(N \times N\) grid; \(\mathcal{S}_2\) is \(n_{\text{det}}\) of them, and the detector is usually the wider aperture of the two. Both are resident. For the 23 196-atom FePt nanoparticle of the BiP-PRISM paper (936² grid, PRISM factor 4), the exact dual-matrix calculation needs 63.18 GB of GPU memory. That is more than any consumer card, and more than most cluster cards.

Partitioning both matrices#

Beam partitioning attacks exactly this. Neighbouring beams in the aperture differ only by a small tilt, so their columns, once de-tilted, are locally similar. Build the matrix on a sparse set of \(B_p \ll B\) parent beams, and reconstruct the column you need for any beam by natural-neighbour interpolation of the parents — de-tilt, combine with the interpolation weights, re-tilt.

The reconstruction is only ever needed locally, and that is the point. The ionization operator \(H_{n0}(\mathbf{r} - \boldsymbol{\tau})\) has sub-ångström support around the atom at \(\boldsymbol{\tau}\), so it is the crop: the columns only have to be right inside a small window, never across the whole grid.

BiP-PRISM — bi-partitioned PRISM — applies this to both matrices at once: \(\mathcal{S}_1\) on parent aperture beams, \(\mathcal{S}_2\) on parent detector beams, each reconstructed on the crop window around the ionized atom immediately before the contraction

\[ \big[\mathcal{S}H_{n0}\big]_{db} \;=\; \sum_{\mathbf{r}\,\in\,\Omega_\tau} \mathcal{S}_{2,d}(\mathbf{r})\,H_{n0}(\mathbf{r}-\boldsymbol{\tau})\,\mathcal{S}_{1,b}(\mathbf{r}) . \]

Both reconstructions resolve before the contraction, on the window, and the window is small — so the resident matrices shrink from \(B\) and \(n_{\text{det}}\) columns to \(B_p\) each, while the arithmetic that consumes them is unchanged.

Resolved detector output

prism_transition_potential retains the full exit diffraction pattern with ordinary multislice on the exit leg, so its partition option applies to \(\mathcal{S}_1\) alone. prism_eels_image reconstructs both matrices and can also retain detector coordinates: use qeels_axis=0 or 1 for a momentum projection, or qeels_axis="both" for individual detector beams. PrismEelsSimulator.qeels calls it this way. Averaging these resolved patterns over probe positions gives core-loss-filtered PACBED. Summing the retained detector intensities recovers the elemental map.

Accuracy, and the one caveat worth knowing#

Partitioning approximates the scattering columns on the inelastic crop window. For a double-partitioned calculation, both the probe and detector reconstruction errors matter. Increase partition and partition_s2 parent counts independently and compare with an unpartitioned calculation at the same grid, PRISM factor, energy window and detector aperture. This isolates partitioning error; convergence of the grid, slice thickness and crop is a separate check.

Since version 0.3, the code uses the corrected carrier signs of the detector reconstruction. Detector columns start from conjugate plane waves, so their de-tilt and re-tilt signs are opposite to those of probe columns. It also removes each parent’s known quadratic vacuum propagation phase (detector de-chirp, on by default) before interpolation and restores the target beam’s phase afterwards. These scalar factors work directly on the crop window. With all beams selected as parents, reconstruction recovers the unpartitioned columns; in vacuum, the corrected detector interpolation is exact even with sparse parents, up to floating-point error.

mag_preserve=True additionally uses the interpolated magnitudes and the phase of the complex natural-neighbour sum on crop windows. This is a heuristic for limiting amplitude loss, not a guarantee of absolute cross-section accuracy. Set mag_preserve=False for plain complex interpolation. For a detector-phase ablation, partition_s2=dict(n_radial=4, dechirp=False) keeps the corrected carrier signs but disables scalar vacuum-phase removal.

Focal referencing#

For prism_eels_image and PrismEelsSimulator.eels_map, focal_backprop=None is the default: interpolate at the current ionization plane. This differs from zero incident-probe defocus, which sets the probe’s entrance phase. Optional focal_backprop="centroid" references the probe parents by half the traversed depth, rounded to a slice, then propagates the interpolated columns forward to the ionization plane. A numeric value specifies the fraction of the traversed depth. Windowed forward propagation uses padding; its truncation must be converged separately. When every beam is a parent, the reference-plane round trip is skipped to preserve the exact cropped columns. No reference plane is assumed to minimize interpolation error universally.

The corrected large-map examples use zero incident defocus with focal back-propagation disabled. The single-column regime survey uses the explicit centroid setting. Scalar detector dechirping is independent of this spatial reference-plane operation.

The elastic operator PrismScanOp has its own default, focal_backprop="centroid". There, “centroid” is the scattering-weighted centroid of the specimen, not half the traversed depth. See Elastic PRISM.

Memory and runtime#

Partitioning reduces resident scattering-matrix storage by replacing full beam sets with parent sets. The per-atom coupling and scan synthesis still cost time; reconstruction overhead can outweigh build savings on small problems. Measure both runtime and peak memory for the specimen and output mode of interest.

contract="columns" forms the beam-to-beam coupling before scan synthesis; contract="probe" forms the local probe first. Both compute the same observable within floating-point precision. The default, contract="auto", picks the order for each atom from the array shapes. The test suite checks that "auto" agrees with both forced orders to a relative tolerance of 1e-11 in float64. v0.3 used "columns"; pass it to force that order. scan_chunk bounds the number of scan positions processed together in the resolved-output and probe-contraction paths; it does not change the output definition.

reconstruction_workspace (bytes) bounds the extra memory that a partitioned call may use to reconstruct one full-grid matrix per slice and crop it for every atom, instead of reconstructing each atom’s window. It applies only when the windows of one slice cover the grid and the full-grid reconstruction fits the budget. None (default) allows 2 GiB. 0 turns the reuse off: this is the windowed path of the paper and of v0.3, with the smallest peak memory. The output does not depend on the value beyond rounding. At the paper’s map-scaling settings the reuse never applies. There, both values give 0.545 GB peak memory and 3.7 s at a 384² grid, and 3.60 GB and 24.6 s at 1024² (complex64, RTX PRO 6000, full float32 matmul precision).

The float32 speed depends on the matmul precision. v0.3 set TF32 ("medium") at import; v0.4 does not. scatterem.configure(matmul_precision="medium") restores the v0.3 speed; see the cost of a call.
