The method#

A 4D-STEM dataset holds more information than any single reconstruction recovers. Direct ptychography reads the phase gradient encoded in the bright-field disk and recovers low and mid spatial frequencies with high efficiency, but its transfer falls away past the disk. Tilt-corrected dark field reads the parallax shift of scattering outside the disk and recovers high frequencies, but carries almost nothing at low ones. Neither channel spans the band on its own, and used alone each leaves a gap where the other is strong.

Fused full-field STEM combines them as an SSNR-weighted Wiener fusion, so each spatial frequency is taken from whichever channel measures it better. The result is one image with no missing band.

Two channels, two halves of the band#

A converged probe illuminates a specimen and a pixelated detector records the full diffraction pattern at every scan position. What reaches the detector splits naturally in two, and the split is where the method starts.

Inside the bright-field disk, the transmitted beam interferes with itself. Weak-phase scattering shifts the intensity distribution across the disk in proportion to the local phase gradient, which is what single-side-band ptychography reads. That works beautifully at low and mid spatial frequencies — each frequency is measured by a pair of overlapping beams, and the overlap is large — but the overlap shrinks as the spatial frequency approaches twice the convergence angle, and past it there is none. Transfer falls to zero.

Outside the disk, scattering is incoherent to good approximation, and a dark-field detector segment sees an image that shifts with defocus and with the segment’s angle — a parallax. Correcting those shifts and summing recovers high spatial frequencies well. But a dark-field segment carries almost no low-frequency information: at low frequency the parallax shift is small and the signal is dominated by thickness and composition rather than phase.

So each channel is strong exactly where the other is weak. Neither spans the band.

Fusing them#

The fusion is a Wiener combination in the Fourier domain. Writing \(P(\mathbf{q})\) and \(D(\mathbf{q})\) for the two channels’ spectra and \(\mathrm{SSNR}_P, \mathrm{SSNR}_D\) for their spectral signal-to-noise ratios,

\[ F(\mathbf{q}) \;=\; \frac{\mathrm{SSNR}_P(\mathbf{q})\, P(\mathbf{q}) + \mathrm{SSNR}_D(\mathbf{q})\, D(\mathbf{q})} {\mathrm{SSNR}_P(\mathbf{q}) + \mathrm{SSNR}_D(\mathbf{q})} \]

which is the minimum-variance linear estimator when the two channels’ noise is independent. Each frequency is taken from whichever channel measures it better, with the crossover placed by the data rather than by a chosen cutoff. Where one SSNR vanishes the sum reduces to the other channel; where both are finite the result is better than either.

The SSNR estimates are what make this more than a heuristic blend, and they are measured, not assumed: the scan is split into two interleaved half-sets in a checkerboard, each is reconstructed independently, and their difference gives the noise power while their mean gives the signal power, frequency by frequency. That costs two reconstructions per channel instead of one, and it means the weights adapt to dose, thickness and specimen without a parameter to tune.

Sub-pixel accumulation#

Tilt-corrected dark field shifts each azimuthal segment by its parallax shift and accumulates. How that shift is applied matters at low dose, and there are two options.

shift_method="fourier" zero-pads and applies a phase ramp. It is exact for dense, band-limited signals.

shift_method="drizzle" (the default) treats each input pixel as a small square drop, places it at the shifted position, and distributes its flux to the output pixels it overlaps by area — the drizzle of Fruchter & Hook (2002), specialised to pure translations on a regular grid. Because every weight is non-negative, the result is non-negative for non-negative input, and flux is conserved exactly.

The difference shows up when the frames are sparse. At very low dose a segment image is a handful of single electron counts, and Fourier shifting turns each isolated count into a sinc: roughly 20% negative side-lobes, with energy spread across the whole frame. Drizzle has no ringing to spread.

Note

The paper’s figures were produced with the Fourier path; drizzle came afterwards. The figure scripts in experiments/ff_stem/ use the drizzle default. Both paths are available. On the amorphous carbon of Fig2_carbon.py, v0.4 gives fused images that correlate to 0.993 between the two paths (relative L2 difference 0.066).

What this is not#

The direct ptychography here is non-iterative. It is a single linear inversion of the bright-field intensity, not a phase-retrieval loop, so it has no convergence behaviour, no step size and no initialisation beyond the aberration estimate. That is why it runs in under a second on a 96² scan. It also means it inherits the weak- phase approximation: strong scatterers and thick specimens depart from it, and an iterative multislice reconstruction will do better there.
